Information Asymmetry

Most mutually beneficial exchanges never happen. Agents change the reason why.

A hospital spends eighteen months designing a trial a pharma company ran two years ago. Two labs approach the same problem from opposite directions, each three years from where the other already stands, and neither ever learns the other exists. These are losses of things that were never made, deals not struck, work not shared, discoveries arrived at twice. 

We blame this on information asymmetry and treat it as human nature. Around it we've built an enormous, expensive infrastructure: lawyers, brokers, escrow, due diligence, etc. to let two parties exchange something they'd both benefit from exchanging, slowly and at great cost, without either getting robbed.

The textbook story is that two parties can't trust each other enough to cooperate, due to competing incentives (mainly money). Underneath is something sharper: the cost of the reveal.

To show you it's valuable, I usually have to show you the information itself, and once you've seen it, you no longer need to buy it. I can't sell you my secret by telling you my secret. The demonstration is the theft.

So these parties mostly aren't failing to find each other. They're failing because revealing is irreversible and they can't know, in advance, that the other side will use what's revealed for the deal rather than against them. Once I show you my position, I can't un-show it. 

Why we never fixed it

The reveal was unsafe for a structural reason, not a technical one: the thing evaluating your information was the same thing that profited from stealing it.

When two humans negotiate, the negotiator is the principal. The mind that sees your secret is the mind that benefits from misusing it – and that mind can't be made to forget, can't be audited, can't be structurally stopped from defecting. Every reason information markets stay thin traces back to this. The same fact explains why negotiation is sequential (humans take turns, and turns leak advantage) and lossy (we reveal a slice because we can't hold the whole thing in our heads). The entire game theory of bargaining is built on the limits of the meat.

So the obstacle isn't a law of nature. It's a law of humans.

Agents solve.

Put an agent in the middle as a change to the structure of the game.

When my agent shows my position to your agent inside a sealed computation, the thing evaluating my information is no longer the thing that profits from stealing it. Your agent computes whether a deal exists and returns one bit (yes or no) without you ever seeing my position. Evaluation and theft come apart. The mind that would profit from the secret never holds it. For humans, decoupling was impossible.

The sequencing collapses too: Two agents don't take turns, they run one symmetric computation across every possible version of the deal at once. There's no first mover because there's no "first." If an overlap exists between what one will offer and what the other will accept, it surfaces to both at the same instant. If it doesn't, the search ends and nothing is revealed.

For all of history, trust lived in relationships and institutions (reputation, repeated games, the lawyer, the broker, the court) because you couldn't verify the counterparty directly, so you paid intermediaries to make betrayal costly. When agents transact, trust can move out of the relationship and into the protocol. You don't trust the counterparty; you trust that the computation was sealed and symmetric. 

There's a catch that's also the opportunity. Autonomous agents open a new gap between me and my own agent, and between my agent and yours. Is the thing I'm negotiating with faithful to its principal, or compromised? That problem didn't exist when the principal was in the room, it gets worse as autonomy grows, and nobody has built the infrastructure for it. That gap is where the rails get built.
